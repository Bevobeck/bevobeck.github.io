Keep students’ thinking visible

I studied how generative AI can affect critical thinking in high school and proposed a teacher-guided lesson process. Three skills I developed

  1. Prompt design. I learned to state the goal, audience, and useful context before asking for a response.
  2. Adapting to the task. I learned that a simple explanation and a classroom activity need different instructions.
  3. Review and revision. When a first answer missed the task’s goal, I learned to identify what was missing and change the prompt.

The specialization gave me a foundation. My next step is to test an AI-assisted classroom activity during teacher preparation and check it before student use.

04 Future of teaching

New tools. Enduring responsibility.

AI can help educators plan and adapt materials. It can also produce answers that are inaccurate, unfair, or wrong for a student. The question I want to keep asking is simple: does this actually help someone learn?

01

Start with the learning goal

Decide what students need before choosing a tool. Use technology where it supports that goal.

02

Check what the tool produces

Review facts, bias, clarity, and student fit. Protect student information and follow school rules.

03

Keep the teacher accountable

Learn as tools change, ask for feedback, and make the final teaching decision with students in mind.

This approach reflects current guidance from the U.S. Department of Education and UNESCO’s teacher AI framework.
